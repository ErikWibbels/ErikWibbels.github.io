Week 3 Assignment

Complete the tasks below and write up your findings in a 1–2 page (single-space) write-up. Remember: graphs and figures do not count against your page limit. Remember to always 'look' at your data, meaning you should ask AI to show you its basic descriptive characteristics. Assignment is due at 5p on Tuesday, September 15.

  1. Return to your survey data. As closely as possible identify questions in the survey (and variables in the data) that measure individual- or household-level labor supply, capital, technology and human capital. A measure of labor supply might be number of hours worked over a week, whether or not the head of household is employed, or whatever. Capital could be measured with household savings or tools used for employment or the quality of a home’s roof (this is very rough, but imagine that a roof can be turned into cash if the house is sold) or whatever. Technology might be access to a cell phone. Etc. Etc.
    1. Justify each of your measures.
    2. Calculate the bivariate correlation between each of these measures (labor, capital, etc.) and the measure of poverty that you calculated last week (Note: you can improve on your measure from last week, if you want. But if you do, explain it!). If you don't know what a 'bivariate correlation' is, ask AI.
    3. Graph the relationship between each of these measures (labor, capital, etc.) and the measure of poverty. Ask AI to plot a regression line through each of these graphs and provide the graphs. If you don’t know what a ‘regression line’ is, ask AI.
      1. Discuss the relationships you see. How strong is the relationship among these measures?
      2. Reflect on the relationships you see in the graphs and discuss: Could there be good reasons that a linear regression would NOT be a good fit, i.e. that non-linear relationships might exist between labor, capital, etc. and your poverty measure?
    4. Meet three households: Pull three specific records (respondents) out of your data, by ID, that look strange/unusual in terms of their relationship between capital/labor/tech and poverty. Given other questions available in the survey and what you’ve learned so far, what do you think might be going on with these households? Remember: this is the part of the assignment that turns a data file back into people.
  2. Use Gapminder’s bubble graphing tool to examine the cross-national relationship between GDP growth (not GDP per capita) and measures of labor supply, capital, technology and human capital. You can explore the data they have available by clicking on the variable on the y-axis and the x-axis — it will open up a menu of different topics and measures. Note that extreme values for one or two countries can make these graphs deceptive — to deal with that, you can play around with the “OPTIONS” button on the right-hand side to exclude extreme cases.
    1. Justify your measures of labor supply, capital, technology and human capital.
    2. Where is your country in the cross-national relationship between each of these measures and GDP growth? Discuss why you think they might be where they are in each relationship.
    3. Press the ‘play’ button at the bottom of each graph (i.e. the graph between GDP per capita and labor, the graph between GDP growth and capital, etc.) to explore the relationship over time. Has your country’s position in the cross-country relationship changed in any significant way? Why/why not?
  3. The Weil textbook reading for this week suggests that labor, capital, technology and human capital cause economic growth. Given 1–3 above, you have now looked at the relationship among these variables. Reflect on — and discuss — the difficulty of imputing causation to your measures of labor supply, capital, technology and human capital.
  4. Don’t do this before the previous step: Without any additional prompting, ask AI to assess the causal relationship between your measures of labor, capital, human capital, technology and household well-being in your data. What does it say? And how do you assess what it says?
