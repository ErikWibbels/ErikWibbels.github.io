Social Policy: Education and Wellbeing

Return to your household survey data.

  • Provide basic information on: a) the mean level of education in your survey; and b) the distribution of education in your survey (you pick the distributional measure that you are most interested in: the standard deviation; the 90-10 ratio; a Gini coefficient of educational attainment; or something else).
    • If educational attainment is not available in your survey, use health outcomes, health access, security outcomes, access to police, or justify some other non-income or expenditure-based measure of wellbeing that is in your survey.
  • Plot the kernel density of educational attainment in your survey. A kernel density is similar to a histogram, but smoothed.
  • Imagine that you stopped your education now.
    • Where would that put you in the distribution of educational attainment in your society?
    • How much does the average person with your number of years of education earn in your place? Is that a living wage in your place or not?
    • If you are working with health or security or labor market data: take your own health or labor market information to your country’s household data.

Find three rows in your data that are distinctive with respect to the relationship between education (or health/security/labor) and economic outcomes—maybe they have lots of education and little food to eat, or they have very good economic outcomes but little access to personal security.
