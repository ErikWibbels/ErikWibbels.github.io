Week 10 Assignment

Turn in your assignment by Tuesday, 10/27 at 5p.

This assignment has three goals:

  1. to get you to think very precisely about how you will measure the outcome you are targeting;
  2. to make informed estimates of what kind of effect size you might reasonably expect your intervention to produce (i.e., how much you think your outcome measure might change as a result of the treatment); and
  3. to use the information from 1 and 2 to inform how large your impact evaluation has to be in order for you to be able to detect a reasonable effect size.

1. Measuring Your Outcome

Once you have defined the outcome you want to impact (high-quality calorie consumption, teacher attendance, household savings, entrepreneurial activity, bribe paying, whatever!), you have to figure out exactly how you will measure it. Your measure could be on a 1–5 scale or a 1–10 scale or a 1–100 scale or a share of workdays missed in a month, or whatever. Think carefully—you want to make sure your measure is as clean and precise as possible; the noisier your measure, the harder it is to distinguish an intervention’s impact on it. Oftentimes there is a standardized way to measure outcomes, so the best way to choose one is to review related studies. Of course, it’s possible the standard measure is bad—for instance, if it suffers from social desirability bias—in which case you should propose something better!

Write up:

  1. the outcome you are trying to impact;
  2. how you will measure it; and
  3. a justification for why this is a good measure (and perhaps why another standard measure is not).

2. Estimate Effect Size

The impact of your intervention is its “effect size.” Your intervention should aim to improve whatever outcome you are targeting, and your measure of the outcome should be able to detect that change. Maybe you expect your healthy eating intervention to increase ‘good’ caloric consumption by 200 calories per day, or maybe you expect your anti-corruption intervention to reduce the incidence of corrupt contracting by 15%—the units, of course, will depend on your measure. But how much might your intervention improve the outcome? You shouldn’t assume massive, unreasonably large effects. Your job is to consider what a “small” effect would be and what a “large” effect would be. Do not guess! Use related studies from your country and/or similar countries to inform your estimate of small and large effects.

Write up: your estimates of small and large effects, including a justification of each and references to the studies or sources that informed your estimates.

3. Use a Power/Sample Size Calculator to Inform the Size of Your Impact Evaluation

“Power” refers to the capacity to measure an effect, if there is one to be found. See J-PAL’s Six Rules of Thumb for a very gentle introduction to statistical power and why it is so important for evaluating your proposed intervention. Then see their quick guide to power calculations.

You will use a power calculator to figure out how large a sample you need in order to detect: (a) a small effect of your intervention; and (b) a large effect of your intervention.

Simple design (treatment vs. control)

If you have a simple, straightforward design that involves comparing treatment and control groups, use this calculator: sample-size.net/sample-size-means. You will see a table that looks like this:

Please keep the top three red entries as they are; we can accept these as standard practice (but do try to understand what they mean!). You will play with the bottom two entries.

  • E is the size of the effect you expect, i.e., the difference between the treatment and control group. Typing an entry here requires that you have done steps 1 and 2 above.
  • S is the standard deviation of the outcome; you should experiment with it as well. The standard deviation reflects the variance in the outcome in the population you are studying. As with effect size, the best way to generate informed guesses as to S is to review related studies (see the J-PAL guide!).

Once you have an informed guess, press the “Calculate” button, and it will tell you how many observations you will need in treatment and control, i.e., the size of your experiment. Be reasonable: if the calculation requires 300,000 units in treatment and control, the experiment might not be feasible, and you will need to rethink.

Cluster randomized design

If you have a cluster randomized design (i.e., you are treating students in schools, patients in clinics, or most other cases where the unit of randomization is above the individual), use this calculator: sample-size.net/means-sample-sizeclustered.

Fill in the initial screen using the guidance above. Then scroll down the page to “Step 2” and fill out the table that looks like this:

  • Fill in C with the number of clusters (schools or clinics or whatever) you might have in the treatment group.
  • Also experiment with filling in ρ, the “intra-cluster correlation” (ICC). You can think of this as how closely correlated outcomes are within clusters. If this number goes up, it means that (for example) children’s school outcomes are more closely correlated within schools; if it goes down, it means the opposite. Why might it go up or down? If schools are locally funded and there is a lot of residential sorting, then the ICC will go up (think to yourself: why?). On the other hand, if education is nationally funded and quality is consistent across schools, the ICC will go down (again: why?). Again, the best way to generate informed guesses as to ρ is to have a look at related studies.

Press “Calculate,” and it will provide you with the number of students (or whatever) per cluster that you need to include in your experiment. You will see that Ntotal, i.e., the size of your experiment, is larger than if you didn’t have a cluster randomized design.

Alternatively, if you have a fixed number of clusters, you can choose “Option B” lower down the page, which looks similar.

Write up:

  1. a justification for your estimate of S, the standard deviation, including references to the relevant studies;
  2. if appropriate, a justification for your estimated intra-cluster correlation; and finally,
  3. a description of the sample size you need to detect both a ‘small’ and a ‘large’ effect.

Think hard about how to measure your outcome! Look at related studies! And experiment with the calculator!
