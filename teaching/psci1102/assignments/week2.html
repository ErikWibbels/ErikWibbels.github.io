Week 2 Exercise

Conceptualizing and Measuring Poverty in Your Country

Due: Tuesday, 9/8 @ 5pm on Canvas

Your assignment is to develop and defend a measure of “poverty” in your country, check how it compares to alternative measures, and look at the specific people your measure sorts into “poor” and “not poor” and say whether the sorting makes sense. By the end you should be able to answer: Why did I choose the poverty concept I chose? What does poverty look like in my place, who bears it, and how confident am I in the number I just produced?

What to do

  1. Define the poverty concept you want to work with. As we’ve talked about in class, poverty can occur across many dimensions: income, wealth (or assets), expenditures, consumption, access to health care or clean water, caloric intake, personal insecurity, voice in politics, financial access, etc. Your choice will be limited by the kinds of questions asked in the survey you are working with, but it should reflect your interests and knowledge of the country context. Not all poverty measures are equally valuable: a poverty measure based on access to a credit card in South Sudan, for instance, isn’t going to be very informative since almost no one has a credit card (indeed, only about 6% of people have a bank account).
  2. Build a measure (and a runner-up). Compute (with AI or stat software) one poverty measure for your country — caloric, income, consumption, educational, asset-based, whatever fits your poverty concept. This requires taking the concept you developed above and translating it into the relevant question(s) in the survey. Then sketch a second measure you seriously considered. You don’t have to fully build out the runner-up, but I want you to consider how it would differ and why you went with your first choice.
  3. Describe the distribution. How much poverty is there, and how is it distributed? Break it down by at least two subgroups that matter in your country (gender, region, urban/rural, ethnicity, age). What do you learn from the subgroup breakdown?
  4. Sanity-check against the world. Find an authoritative poverty figure for your country — World Bank, your national statistics office, UNDP’s MPI — and compare it to what you got. They probably won’t match. Try to figure out why: different poverty concept? Different line? Consumption vs. income survey? Survey weights you did or didn’t apply? Currency conversion? A year mismatch? If you can’t explain the gap, keep digging.
  5. Meet three households. Pull three specific records (respondents) out of your data, by ID, that your measure treats in interesting ways. Suggested: one your measure calls poor, one it calls non-poor but that sits right at the line, and one where your measure and your gut disagree. For each, tell me what the data says about that household and whether the “poor” label is justified. This is the part of the assignment that turns a data file back into people.
  6. Red-team a chatbot. Ask AI to define and compute a poverty measure for your country from your dataset. Take apart what it produces: how does it differ from your measure? What did it get wrong or gloss over for your specific place? Maybe it defaulted to a dollar-a-day line that’s meaningless for your country’s economy, ignored in-kind income, quietly dropped the households with missing data, or picked variables that don’t mean what it assumed. If it did well, say where — and say how you checked.

A strong submission is one in which you clearly justify your choices, your poverty estimate has been checked against an external source, the three households are discussed like people rather than rows, and the AI critique shows you can catch a confident AI being wrong or simplistic about your country.
