Course Description

Why are some countries rich and some poor? Why are some households rich and some poor? This course introduces students to the analytical tools for understanding why development varies across the world and the practical tools for designing and evaluating policies aimed at alleviating poverty. To that end, the course is organized into two parts. The first part focuses on the micro-picture: the household-level dynamics of poverty and development, including access to food and credit, the role of health and education, migration from village to city life, and day-to-day governance. You will learn the practicalities of designing and evaluating the efficacy of governance and poverty relief interventions and policies. The focus will be on the use of field experiments to study interventions to promote better governance and household wellbeing. The second part of the course focuses on the big picture: the macroeconomic and political foundations for sustained economic growth, including historical legacies, technological innovation and political institutions.

The class will be taught with an eye toward gathering both analytical and practical skills. To promote the latter, we will use the last 20 minutes of Thursday meetings to work on exercises and activities that will develop your ability to work with data, develop country expertise, design field experiments, and evaluate governance and anti-poverty programs. In doing this work, the underlying data will come from a country that you care about, and your applied research on caloric consumption, social mobility, etc. will come from your own experience. Thus, the hard skills you will be developing will be deployed on places, issues and people that you care about. Since these assignments will take some preparation on your part, I have taken it easy with the reading load for the class.

This course is the first in a two-course sequence; the second is PSCI 3200 – Global Development: Intermediate Topics in Politics, Policy, and Data, which is taught in the spring. Both courses are required for the minor in International Development.

Course Requirements

Midterm (25% of final grade): The midterm will test the material covered in the first half of the class. Exams will be a mixture of short-answer and essay questions. Material covered in both the lectures and the readings are fair game. I will provide a comprehensive review sheet in advance. The midterm is Monday, 10/12.

Final Exam (25% of final grade): The final will be identical in format to the midterm, but it will test information covered since the midterm. Its date/time/location is TBD.

Weekly exercises (25% of final grade): Each week’s exercise will involve collecting or working with data, learning some detailed information about your chosen country, or research on your family history of social mobility. This preparation will include a 1–2 page report, plus any tables or figures you might generate; the report might involve an explanation of the figures you have generated, an accounting of how much your food consumption costs, or some ideas on why your country does or does not fit standard relationships between wealth/poverty and health, institutional quality, governance, etc. We will discuss these exercises for the last 20 minutes of each Wednesday class.

In order to conduct these exercises, you will need to choose a country to serve as your laboratory this semester. You should be curious about this place since you’ll be doing regular research on it. You will need to find a household survey or census data of your country; you will need to work with a dataset containing individual records, i.e. information collected on persons and/or households.

Historically this course required students to work in R or Python. Given advancements in AI, I expect most applied data work to be done with AI in the future. Thus, you will have the option of doing the data work using software or AI of your choosing. If you want to use R or Python, they are downloadable for free and have enormous online help support.

The goals of these weekly exercises are to learn something about original data collection, how to engage with data as a critical consumer, and how to use it to gain a richer understanding of the people represented by the data. These are not things that AI can give you, even if it can be enormously helpful in the finding and analysis of data. To that end, assignments will typically require these components:

  • The result: Your figure, table, or number, with a few sentences on what it shows about your place.
  • The decision log: A short accounting of the choices you made and the ones you rejected: which variables you considered, how you handled missing values and why, what you recoded and on what logic, and — importantly — what you tried first that turned out to be wrong. I would rather see an honest record of a dead end than a clean result you can’t explain. When your concept (say, “poverty”) could be built several ways, tell me why you chose yours and what a defensible alternative would have looked like.
  • A sanity check: Wherever an official or published figure exists — a World Bank poverty headcount, a national statistics office number, a UN estimate — compute your own version and compare. If they disagree, your job is to explain the gap (weights, poverty line, PPP conversion, coverage, or a genuine data problem). Getting a number is easy; knowing whether it’s right is the skill.

Two practices you should pursue across weekly assignments:

  • Meet the households — Often I’ll ask you to leave the summary statistics and pull a handful of actual records out of your microdata — specific households or individuals — and reason about them by ID. Is household 4,471 poor? Why do two families with the same income look so different on other dimensions? This is where the data stops being a spreadsheet and starts being people in a place.
  • Build on your own prior work. Because you return to the same country every week, your reports accumulate into something only you hold. Later assignments will ask you to reconcile what you found earlier — where does the household story fit, or clash, with the growth story? Your final project will draw explicitly on this record.

You can use my office hours for help with your weekly assignment, but the Research Data team at Penn Libraries also has more extensive weekly office hours and/or you can schedule a meeting with them.

Your weekly reports should be turned in via Canvas by 5p Tuesdays. Any assignment turned in late will be subject to the late assignment policy outlined below. I will post the assignments on Canvas a week ahead of time. In addition to instructions, I will include references, some R code, an example, etc. to help guide the way.

Final Project (25% of final grade): The weekly exercises will build toward you designing a governance or poverty alleviation program in your country, region, or city. You will justify your program design and provide a research design for evaluating whether or not the program works. These final project reports should be 10–12 pages. I will provide a detailed handout on this assignment later in the semester, and we will talk about it throughout the semester. The final project is due on Friday, December 11 at midnight; it also includes a required, graded 15-minute interview with me about the project on Monday or Tuesday, December 14/15.

Participation (0 percent): This class will be taught in a combination lecture/seminar format. Even when I’m lecturing, I would encourage you to interrupt, ask questions, raise points, and generally poke and prod. As such, it is essential that you come to class having done the readings and be prepared to discuss them. Historically, class participation has been strongly correlated with grades even though I don’t take attendance.

Policies

No makeup exams beyond restrictive University rules (illness, religious holiday, etc.). If a makeup is absolutely necessary, contact me before the day of the exam.

No late assignments. All late assignments will be punished by one letter grade for every day they are late. In other words, an “A” becomes a “B”, if the paper is one day late; the “B” becomes a “C” after two days, etc.

All appeals of grades received on an exam or assignment need to be submitted no later than two working days after the date they are returned to the class. Please provide me with a written statement explaining precisely why you think a grade change is in order. Note that in the event of an appeal I will re-grade your entire exam/paper under the assumption that if I was heavy-handed on one part of the exam/paper, I might have been excessively tolerant elsewhere. Your grade might go up, but it could go down.

Plagiarism and cheating are violations of academic integrity and thus violations of the Penn Code of Academic Integrity. Violations could result in a variety of disciplinary actions, including suspension or permanent dismissal from the University. For the purposes of this class, plagiarism will be defined as submitting a piece of work which in part or in whole is not entirely the student's own work without attributing those same portions to their correct source. If you are at all unsure as to what constitutes plagiarism, see the University's academic integrity website. I will report any perceived violation to the university for investigation.

Use of AI: Large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and the rest) are now good enough to do the analysis for most of these assignments and produce a plausible-looking poverty measure or correlation in seconds. Pretending otherwise would be silly. But a model that does the analysis for you can also do your thinking for you, and that trade is a bad deal when the understanding is the thing you’re here to build. So the rule in this class is: use AI but be open about it:

  • You can use AI freely for background research and data analysis. If you use it for either purpose, cite it: note the tool, the date, and how you used it.
  • For the substantive choices — how to define poverty for your country, how to treat missing or extreme values, which survey questions map to your concept, whether a number is believable — the work has to be yours. That judgment is exactly what the course is teaching and what your final project will test. AI can’t tell you what you mean by poverty in your place.
  • On some assignments I’ll ask you to do the opposite of hiding your AI use: generate a first-pass answer with a model, then red-team it, meaning your job is to confront its output — where it made a bad call for your specific context, where it didn’t know that most income in your country is in-kind, or that your survey top-codes assets, or that its “standard” recode buries the households you care about. The critique is the assignment. On other assignments I’ll ask you to provide your ‘prompt engineering,’ i.e. to explicitly provide the logic that drove your instructions for AI. These capacities — to provide analytically useful instructions to, and critically evaluate the output of, AI — are likely the most valuable data skill you’ll leave here with.
